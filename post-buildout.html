All-you-can-eat compute

And other arguments with a chip investor: the monopoly that forgot to charge more, the bear case I failed to defend, and whose robots get to learn.

May 27, 2026

A few weeks ago I spent an afternoon with a friend who invests in semiconductors for a living. It was a fair trade: I work inside the industry he prices, and he prices the industry I work inside.

The demand side of AI currently eats like it's at an all-you-can-eat buffet — the largest hyperscalers have committed on the order of $650 billion of capex for 2026 alone, and AI investment has lately added more to US GDP growth than all consumer spending combined. Everything we argued about that afternoon reduces to two questions about that buffet. On the supply side: where does the kitchen actually bottleneck, and who captures the money when it does? On the demand side: does the appetite ever plateau — or is the customer base about to get much, much hungrier? And hovering behind both, a third question we couldn't resist: what gets served next.

That's why Bruegel's Tower of Babel sits at the top of this essay. The buffet is the demand side; the tower is the supply side — the grandest construction project anyone had ever attempted, painted mid-build, with nobody in the frame quite sure it can be finished.

1. The monopoly that forgot to charge more

Ask an outsider to name a semiconductor company and you'll hear NVIDIA, maybe AMD; the better-read will bring up ASML and its lithography monopoly. Now look at what actually went vertical this cycle. The day before we met, Micron crossed a $1 trillion market cap — the stock is up more than 850% over the trailing year. SanDisk is up over 4,000% in the same stretch. These are businesses locked in brutal competition, repricing like meme stocks. The textbook monopolies had years most companies would kill for — ASML and TSMC are both up around 110% — and next to memory they still looked like they were standing still.

I work at ASML, so this one isn't academic to me: if you're the only seller of an irreplaceable machine, why on earth aren't you the one raising prices? The answer we converged on: the market this cycle wasn't paying for position — it was paying for pricing structure. Memory sells into something close to a spot market; when the frenzy hit, prices repriced overnight and earnings exploded. Lithography and leading-edge foundry sell through multi-year backlogs and contract-locked prices, and the very structure that makes those monopolies durable also caps their upside. A moat protects you from competitors. It doesn't entitle you to the cycle. In a frenzy, the market pays for velocity; durability gets paid later — if you can hold.

2. I brought a bear case. It didn't survive the afternoon.

My case, honestly held: for most people, AI is a chatbot that runs errands, and the recent generation of models already crossed the threshold that matters to them — a few prompts now produce a respectable website or a small app. Past that point, each new model's marginal utility thins fast for anyone who isn't a prosumer. And outside the model companies themselves, I still can't name an application-layer product that made me gasp. That combination smells like the top of something.

He was having none of it. His counter: we're early, not late. Intelligence is still expensive — a frontier model like Claude Opus runs $5 per million input tokens and $25 per million output, while the budget end of the market, DeepSeek, charges about $0.28 and $0.42. That's an 18-to-60x spread between the intelligence people want and the intelligence people can afford to pour into products. Squeeze even 10% out of the upstream — cost or capability — and the downstream doesn't grow 10%; it grows 100% or 1,000%, because whole categories of products become economical at once. Steam engines got more efficient and coal consumption went up, not down; compute has been running that exact script for sixty years.

His bubble test was refreshingly blunt, too: look at AI's share of the economy. All the data centers, chips, and networking together still amount to something like 1.5% of US GDP. Bubbles pop when an industry is priced as if it were the economy; this one is priced like an appetizer. (The honest footnote: that little sliver is currently doing much of the economy's lifting — which is either early-cycle evidence or concentration risk, depending on your mood.)

I lost this argument, and I'm fine with it. The plateau I kept predicting was about capability; the curve that actually matters is about price. My "good enough" threshold isn't a ceiling — once tokens get cheap enough, it becomes a floor.

One asterisk, though. The logic works; the timing is the bet. "Infra gets 10% cheaper and the app layer explodes" requires the cost curve to actually reach that threshold in the next few years — and tech has a habit of keeping theoretically-inevitable things exactly five years away. Full self-driving has been two years from solved for a decade. Cheap intelligence could pull the same trick.

3. Whose robots get to learn?

On the next frontier we agreed almost instantly: chatbots don't touch most of physical life, which is why physical AI is the obvious next act — and why robotics companies have lately gotten louder in marketing than their technology strictly justifies. No paradigm has converged. Everyone is burning cash. And underneath all of it, the bottleneck is data.

Then the views collided. One side of the table: China wins the data game — collection is cheaper, scale-up is faster, and if domestic lithography ever delivers a stable compute supply, the long-run advantage in AI and robotics tilts east. My side: robots are still far too expensive for consumers, so the demand that pays today is B2B — and the tasks businesses will pay for are the complex, high-stakes ones: logistics, biotech experiments, semiconductor cleanroom work. That data is scarce, hard to collect, and expensive for a reason. The race isn't who collects more data; it's whose data is worth collecting. And the collection and scale-up of that kind of data seems likelier to happen in the US — the high-value tasks are here, and the world's robotics talent keeps buying one-way tickets to Silicon Valley. Density compounds.

We didn't settle it. Good arguments rarely settle; they compound too.

The best part of the afternoon wasn't where we agreed. It was arguing with someone whose conviction has money behind it — it forces your own opinions to sharpen or die. Mine did some of each.
