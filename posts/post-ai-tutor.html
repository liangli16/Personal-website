How I accidentally became an AI tutor

On building one small tool, and the brilliant people it turned out to help.

June 29, 2026

The most useful thing I built at ASML started as a favor. In D&E — ASML's development & engineering org, where I worked — a colleague had worked out beautiful physics for aligning an electron gun — a process that, until then, was done by a human eyeballing a glowing spot and turning knobs. The model was stuck at the theory stage because nobody could reliably measure the spot. I knew computer vision, so I got asked. I built the measurement, then a GUI around it, and alignment went from a craft that took an experienced eye to a number anyone could hit: about ten times more precise, 66% faster. The interesting part, it turns out, wasn't the pipeline. It's everything that happened around it.

The theoretical physicist who couldn't code

Then came the part I never planned. After the tool made the rounds, colleagues started asking me to teach them how I'd built it — not the CV, the workflow. One of them was a theoretical physics PhD, genuinely one of the sharpest people I've met. She could barely code. And why would she? Her PhD predates AI assistants, and her work lived in equations — the occasional light data processing, never real software. Nothing in her career had required it, until suddenly everything did.

Here's what that taught me about elite engineering teams: when everyone's CV glitters, everyone politely assumes that everything is trivial to everyone else. Nobody says "I don't know what git is" in a room full of PhDs and Ivy League degrees. So the gap never surfaces — it just quietly compounds. I taught from absolute zero: what version control is for, what a sane development loop looks like, how to work with AI agents without letting them work you. She and her colleagues went on to ship their own internal tool for a completely different problem, and credited me in the project as their "AI tutor."

I think about that gap a lot now. It's the same gap that exists between every engineering team and its end users — the tool-builders assume fluency, the users hide confusion, and the product quietly fails in the space between. Most technology doesn't fail because it's wrong. It fails because it assumes fluency nobody admits to lacking.

You can't read your way into a new technology

The reason they came to me, specifically: I'd built the interface with AI coding agents, back when using them at work was still an eccentric habit rather than a default. I didn't do it because I'd read that agents were the future. I did it because I was curious, and the only way I trust myself to evaluate a new technology is to ship something real with it.

That conviction hardened during this project. There is an enormous industry of opinions about AI — takes, newsletters, keynote slides — and almost none of it survives contact with an actual deadline. Where agents are miraculous and where they quietly ruin your afternoon: you learn that in your hands, not in your feed. Reading about a tool gives you opinions. Building with one gives you judgment.

What I'm taking with me

Two discoveries, both mildly embarrassing in how much they surprised me:

  1. The fluency gap is everywhere once you learn to see it — between brilliant people from different backgrounds, and just as much between the engineers who design a tool and the people on the production floor who live with it. It isn't a personnel problem; it's a design and teaching problem.
  2. When a technology is new enough that nobody really knows it yet, being willing to just try — to learn by building — pays in ways you can't plan for. Mine paid in an "AI tutor" credit, and in discovering an interest, maybe even a talent, I didn't know I had.

I'm heading into robotics next, where this exact gap is wider than anywhere: extraordinary machines, and a developer experience that still assumes you already know everything. That seam — between hard systems and the people who need them to just work — is where I want to work next.
